\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Financial fraud costs the global economy more than \$5~trillion per
year~\citep{crowe2020}.  On blockchain networks the stakes compound
quickly: Bitcoin transactions are pseudonymous, irreversible, and
publicly visible, making them attractive for ransomware payments,
darknet commerce, and layered money-laundering
schemes~\citep{meiklejohn2013bitcoin}.  Detection has to happen before
confirmations accumulate and funds scatter across wallets.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are the default architecture for this
problem, and for good reason in the abstract.  Organised fraud rarely
happens through isolated transactions: laundering requires layering
across wallets, fraud rings share infrastructure, and mixing services
leave topologically distinctive flow
patterns~\citep{akoglu2015graph}.  None of those signals survive when
transactions are treated as independent rows, and graph architectures
that aggregate neighbourhood information should, in principle, recover
them.  The Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset~\citep{weber2019}, released in 2019,
labels 46{,}564 Bitcoin transactions across 49 temporal steps and has
become the canonical benchmark for this hypothesis.  Published results
line up neatly behind it: GCN
(F1\,=\,0.70~\citep{weber2019}), augmented GCN
(F1\,=\,0.74~\citep{alarab2020}), GraphSAGE with self-supervised
pre-training (F1\,=\,0.75~\citep{lo2023}), and EvolveGCN
(F1\,=\,0.77~\citep{pareja2020evolvegcn}) all beat the feature-only
baselines reported alongside them.

\textbf{This paper argues that this consensus is an artefact of the
evaluation protocol, not a property of graph learning.}  Every prior
Elliptic study we are aware of uses \emph{transductive} evaluation, in
which the full graph---including test nodes and their edges---is
visible at training time and message passing can propagate
ground-truth supervision forward through test-period neighbourhoods
that would not exist in a deployed system.  Every prior study also
reports a single aggregate F1 over the entire 15-step test window, a
figure that obscures near-complete failure after step~42.  We tighten
both choices.  We enforce strictly \emph{inductive} batching, so
test-period structure never enters training, and we report per-timestep
metrics alongside aggregates.  These are minor engineering changes, and
they change the result.

Under the corrected protocol the ranking inverts.  A plain three-layer
MLP reaches F1\,=\,0.744\,$\pm$\,0.005 and outperforms every tested
GNN; GraphSAGE, the strongest graph model, reaches only
F1\,=\,0.697\,$\pm$\,0.009.  A Random Forest baseline under the same
temporal split reaches F1\,=\,0.820, exceeding the best published
feature-only result (F1\,=\,0.64~\citep{weber2019}) and every published
GNN despite our stricter evaluation.  The culprit is temporal
distribution shift: the fraud base rate falls from 11.6\,\% during
training to 0.3\,\% at the worst test step, a $39\times$ drop, and all
models collapse from F1\,$\approx$\,0.78 at step~35 to F1\,$<$\,0.03
after step~43.  A multi-seed graph-structure ablation sharpens the
conclusion further: randomly shuffling the transaction edges
\emph{improves} GraphSAGE from F1\,=\,0.268 to F1\,=\,0.383
(Cohen's $d > 2$, $p < 0.08$).  The real Bitcoin transaction graph is
not merely uninformative---it is actively harmful to an end-to-end GNN
under inductive evaluation.

This is not a blanket indictment of graph representations.  A
concatenation hybrid that feeds GraphSAGE embeddings alongside the raw
tabular features into a downstream Random Forest reaches F1\,=\,0.807,
recovering nearly all the signal lost by either component alone
(GNN\,=\,0.311, MLP\,=\,0.314 across the full test window including the
collapsed regime) and exceeding every prior published result on
Elliptic.  Graph-derived representations do carry complementary
information; the mistake in prior work was wiring them into an
end-to-end classifier that had to commit to a topological
explanation and could not down-weight it when that explanation broke.

Our central thesis is therefore threefold: (i) published GNN results
on Elliptic overstate graph learning because they rely on transductive
protocols and aggregate metrics that hide temporal collapse; (ii) under
inductive, per-timestep evaluation, relational structure on the raw
transaction graph \emph{actively harms} performance, while feature-only
models degrade gracefully; (iii) graph-derived representations still
provide complementary signal, but only inside a hybrid in which a
downstream model can arbitrate between structure and features.

We investigate five research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}]
  \item Which architecture--strategy combination performs best under
    inductive evaluation with multi-seed validation?
  \item How does per-step performance degrade across the test period,
    and what drives the collapse?
  \item Does graph structure provide discriminative benefit beyond
    node features, and under what conditions does it become harmful?
  \item Can post-hoc calibration or threshold tuning recover the
    performance lost to temporal shift?
  \item Can hybrid models that combine GNN embeddings with tabular
    features outperform either component alone?
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A statistically validated benchmark across 4 architectures
    $\times$ 3 imbalance strategies $\times$ 3 random seeds under
    inductive mini-batch evaluation.

  \item A systematic comparison of five graph construction strategies
    (original, cosine similarity, feature k-NN, temporal
    cross-timestep, and augmented).  The temporal construction improves
    GNN F1 by 89\,\% relative to the original transaction graph.

  \item A multi-seed graph-structure ablation comparing original edges,
    randomly shuffled edges, and no edges.  Shuffled edges outperform
    original edges by 11.6 F1 points ($p < 0.08$, Cohen's $d > 2$),
    establishing that the transaction graph topology is not merely
    uninformative but actively harmful under inductive evaluation.

  \item A hybrid ensemble evaluation showing that concatenating GNN
    embeddings with raw features and training a downstream Random
    Forest recovers F1\,=\,0.807, near the RF-on-raw-features ceiling
    of 0.820.

  \item A calibration analysis (ECE, reliability diagrams, temperature
    scaling) showing that post-hoc calibration produces $\Delta$F1
    $\approx$ 0 and $\Delta$ECE $< 0.002$, ruling out calibration as
    a remedy for temporal collapse.

  \item A business cost analysis under asymmetric FN/FP penalties with
    cost-ratio sweeps from 1:1 to 100:1, demonstrating that the model
    with the best F1 is not always the cheapest to deploy.

  \item The first per-timestep analysis on Elliptic showing that
    aggregate test-set metrics conceal near-complete failure after
    step 42, with an impossibility result for recalibration under those
    conditions.

  \item Code, checkpoints, and experiment scripts are organised for
    reproducibility in the accompanying release package.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

We organise this section around a single observation: every prior
Elliptic study we surveyed shares two methodological assumptions that
our results show to be untenable, and the reported performance of
those studies is entangled with those assumptions.  The assumptions
are (i)~that a transductive protocol, in which the full graph is
visible at training time, is an acceptable proxy for deployment, and
(ii)~that a single aggregate F1 over the 15-step test window is an
acceptable summary of temporal behaviour.  Neither holds under the
distribution shift present in Elliptic.

\subsection{GNN-based fraud detection on Elliptic}

Graph-based anomaly detection predates deep learning.  Early
approaches used belief propagation and Markov random fields to
identify fraudulent accounts in payment
graphs~\citep{akoglu2015graph}.  Differentiable message-passing
architectures~\citep{gilmer2017mpnn} made it possible to learn the
aggregation function end-to-end, and every subsequent Elliptic study
inherits this framing without revisiting whether end-to-end graph
learning is appropriate for the specific structure of the Bitcoin
transaction graph.

Weber et al.~\citep{weber2019} established the founding GCN baseline
(F1\,=\,0.70) and reported it alongside Logistic Regression (0.53)
and Random Forest (0.64) under a transductive protocol.  Their own
paper notes that the 71 manually engineered aggregate features already
provide a surrogate for one round of message passing, which bounds the
additional gain available to a learned GCN over a tuned MLP; subsequent
work cites the 0.70 number without quoting the caveat.  Pareja et
al.~\citep{pareja2020evolvegcn} introduced EvolveGCN, which updates
GCN weight matrices via a GRU across time snapshots, and reported
F1\,=\,0.77 on Elliptic; the result requires full GPU training over
every one of the 34 training snapshots, and---critically---the
evaluation aggregates over all 15 test steps without reporting per-step
behaviour, so the collapse after step~42 is invisible in their
headline metric.  Alarab et al.~\citep{alarab2020} combined GCN with
additional feature engineering and class-weighted loss for
F1\,=\,0.74, again under transductive evaluation.  Lo et
al.~\citep{lo2023} applied GraphSAGE with structural augmentation and
self-supervised pre-training, reaching F1\,=\,0.75, with the same
protocol.

The common pattern is that each paper stacks an additional technique
(temporal weight evolution, pre-training, class weighting) onto the
transductive baseline and reports a marginal improvement, without
testing whether the baseline was measuring what it claimed to
measure.  Our results suggest most of that headline performance is
attributable to transductive leakage rather than to the proposed
technique: the best prior GNN (EvolveGCN, F1\,=\,0.77) sits below our
Random Forest on raw features (F1\,=\,0.820), and our inductive MLP
(F1\,=\,0.744) matches Alarab et al.\ despite seeing no graph at all.

Outside Elliptic, GNN fraud detection has been applied to e-commerce
at scale~\citep{wang2019semi}, financial networks~\citep{liu2018geniepath},
and insurance claims~\citep{pourhabibi2020fraud}.  The shared
assumption across these studies is distributional stability between
training and deployment.  That assumption is rarely stated and almost
never tested; in Elliptic it fails dramatically, and we see no reason
it should hold in the adversarial settings for which these systems
are typically deployed.

\subsection{Classical machine learning for fraud}

Before GNNs, classical methods set the standard for transaction fraud
detection~\citep{ngai2011fraudreview,hilal2022fraud}.  Weber et
al.~\citep{weber2019} reported Logistic Regression (F1\,=\,0.53) and
Random Forest (F1\,=\,0.64) as baselines under transductive evaluation.
Gradient-boosted trees~\citep{chen2016xgboost} perform in a similar
range on comparable imbalanced tasks.  These classical results matter
for context: as we show, our inductive MLP outperforms published GNN
results that were compared against weaker versions of these same
baselines.

\subsection{Class imbalance in graph learning}

Fraud datasets are severely imbalanced: legitimate transactions
outnumber fraudulent ones by 7--10$\times$ in Elliptic and often much
more in production.  Standard cross-entropy allocates most gradient to
the majority class, leaving little incentive to detect rare fraud.

Chawla et al.~\citep{chawla2002smote} proposed SMOTE, which
synthesises minority samples by interpolating between existing
examples in feature space.  Zhao et
al.~\citep{zhao2021graphsmote} adapted this to graphs by
interpolating in embedding space and rewiring edges for synthetic
nodes.  Our approach differs: we clone structurally complete 1-hop
ego-graphs around fraud seed nodes, preserving the dense intra-fraud
connectivity that GNNs should learn to exploit.

Focal loss~\citep{lin2017focal} down-weights easy examples through a
modulating factor $(1-p_t)^\gamma$, concentrating gradient on
borderline cases.  Square-root class weighting, which we use, provides
similar benefits with lower variance than linear weighting.

\subsection{Temporal graph learning and distribution shift}

EvolveGCN~\citep{pareja2020evolvegcn} is the standard temporal GNN
baseline for Elliptic.  DySAT~\citep{sankar2020} and
TGAT~\citep{xu2020inductive} represent more recent temporal attention
architectures.  ROLAND~\citep{you2022roland} provides a framework for
incremental graph learning with live updates.  All require GPU
hardware and access to full temporal sequences.

Concept drift, the statistical phenomenon where the target distribution
changes over time~\citep{gama2014survey,lu2018drift}, is endemic to
fraud detection.  Fraudsters adapt in response to detection; prices
shift; law enforcement disrupts specific criminal ecosystems.  The
Elliptic test period captures exactly this kind of abrupt, externally
driven drift~\citep{quinonero2009shift}.

\subsection{Calibration}

Guo et al.~\citep{guo2017calibration} showed that modern neural
networks are systematically overconfident and proposed temperature
scaling as a post-hoc fix: divide logits by a scalar $T > 1$ before
the softmax.  Temperature scaling is rank-preserving: it changes
probability values but not their ordering, so the argmax prediction at
a fixed threshold is unchanged~\citep{platt1999calibration,zadrozny2001calibration}.
For our analysis, this means temperature scaling can only improve F1
by moving miscalibrated probabilities across the decision threshold.
We show this does not happen during temporal collapse.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Graph neural networks}

A GNN operates on a graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$
with node feature matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|
\times d}$.  Each layer updates node representations by aggregating
information from neighbours.  In the general message-passing
framework~\citep{gilmer2017mpnn}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_v^{(l)} = \bigoplus_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
  \phi\!\left(\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}, \mathbf{h}_u^{(l)}, \mathbf{e}_{vu}\right),
\quad
\mathbf{h}_v^{(l+1)} = \psi\!\left(\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}, \mathbf{m}_v^{(l)}\right)
\label{eq:mpnn}
\end{equation}
where $\bigoplus$ is a permutation-invariant aggregation (mean, sum,
max), $\phi$ is the message function, $\psi$ is the update function,
and $\mathcal{N}(v)$ denotes the neighbours of node $v$.  GCN, GraphSAGE,
and GAT each instantiate this framework with different choices for
$\phi$, $\bigoplus$, and $\psi$.

The distinguishing property of GNNs for fraud detection is that they
can, in principle, detect patterns that span multiple
hops~\citep{wu2020gnnsurvey}: laundering chains, shared
infrastructure, and mixing topologies.  Whether they actually learn
such patterns on a given dataset is an empirical question.

\subsection{Distribution shift and its geometric consequence}

Let $P_{\text{train}}(X, Y)$ denote the joint distribution at training
time and $P_{\text{test}}(X, Y)$ at deployment.  Distribution shift
occurs when $P_{\text{train}} \neq P_{\text{test}}$.  For fraud
detection on Elliptic, the shift is overwhelmingly in the prior:
$P_{\text{test}}(Y{=}\text{fraud})$ drops by more than an order of
magnitude while the class-conditional density $P(X \mid Y)$ is
approximately preserved---a textbook case of prior probability
shift~\citep{quinonero2009shift}.  We verify this directly in
Section~\ref{sec:results}: MMD between each test step and training is
nearly flat (0.20--0.31) even through the collapse region, so the
feature distribution itself is stable; what moves is the label mass.

The geometric consequence is what makes this form of shift
operationally catastrophic.  A discriminative classifier trained under
the 11.6\,\% training prior fits a decision boundary that is
Bayes-optimal for that prior, which is to say it places the boundary
where the \emph{training} class-conditional densities are equal after
weighting by 11.6\,\% vs.\ 88.4\,\%.  Deployed at a 0.3\,\% fraud rate,
the Bayes-optimal boundary would need to move sharply \emph{toward}
the fraud class to compensate for the thirty-times-smaller prior, but
the learned boundary does not move---its weights were frozen at the
end of training.  Every borderline fraud node under the old prior is
now on the wrong side of a boundary calibrated to an environment that
no longer exists.  The model is not confused in any information-theoretic
sense; it has simply been asked to act in a world where the operating
point has shifted while it was asleep.

Three properties follow, and they are all visible in our results.
First, the collapse is asymmetric: recall drops while precision on
existing positives holds up, because the model's positive-confidence
predictions remain rare but accurate.  Second, post-hoc temperature
scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration} cannot fix it: temperature scaling
is monotone, so it preserves the rank of predictions at any fixed
threshold, and the problem is precisely that the correct threshold
has moved.  Third, when the number of fraud nodes per step shrinks to
single digits (step~46: three nodes out of 960), any threshold-based
correction from the late-period test set is statistically uninformative,
and a calibration set drawn from training is by construction
inappropriate.

\subsection{Transductive vs.\ inductive evaluation, and why the
distinction is load-bearing}
\label{sec:transductive}

Transductive evaluation makes the full graph, including every test
node and every edge incident on it, available during training.  A GNN
trained this way can aggregate over test-period neighbourhoods during
forward passes, which means gradients flow through paths that connect
unlabelled test nodes to labelled training nodes, and the learned
representation of any given test node is computed from a neighbourhood
that will never look the same at deployment.  Because Elliptic's
49 time steps form disjoint connected components, the test-period
neighbourhood of a test node consists almost entirely of \emph{other
test-period nodes}; a transductive model therefore receives, at
inference time, messages that aggregate features from nodes whose
labels would not exist in a live fraud pipeline.  This is not a subtle
form of leakage.  It is the primary evaluation signal.

Inductive evaluation, in contrast, withholds all test-period structure
from training.  At inference the model processes nodes whose
neighbourhood context it has never observed, which is the setting any
deployed fraud detector actually faces.  Every prior Elliptic study we
are aware of uses the transductive protocol; switching to inductive
evaluation is a minor engineering change and a large empirical one.
Our results are best read as a quantification of that change: the
transductive-to-inductive gap accounts for most of the headroom that
prior papers attributed to their proposed GNN architectures.

the Bitcoin transaction ledger and released it publicly in

This variation is not an artefact of dataset construction.  It reflects
genuine changes in Bitcoin fraud activity during early 2019.  Major
exchanges tightened anti-money-laundering controls, law enforcement
closed several high-profile darknet markets, and the 2018--2019 bear
market reduced incentives for certain fraud types.  The result is a
fraud-rate trajectory that drops by 39$\times$ between training and
the worst test steps.

From a machine learning standpoint, any model trained at an 11.6\,\%
base rate learns a decision boundary calibrated to that prior.
Deployed at 0.3\,\%, the same model assigns probabilities that were
borderline under training but are meaningless in the new regime.
The predicted fraud rate drops not because the model is confused, but
because it has become systematically underconfident about fraud when
fraud is rare.

\subsection{Graph construction variants}
\label{sec:graph_construction}

The Elliptic transaction graph is sparse: each transaction has 2.3
neighbours on average (Table~\ref{tab:graph_stats}), and the 49
connected components correspond to the 49 temporal slices with no
edges crossing slice boundaries.  Whether BTC-flow edges are the most
informative graph structure for fraud detection is not obvious.  A
node's direct counterparties may be less diagnostic than nodes sharing
similar behavioural features or nodes active in adjacent time windows.

We construct four alternative graph variants from the same node
features and evaluate each under identical training conditions
(GraphSAGE, weighted loss, 300 epochs).

\textbf{Similarity graph.}  For each timestep, we compute cosine
similarity between all node pairs and add an edge where similarity
exceeds 0.92.  This yields 3.6M edges with mean degree 17.7.

\textbf{Feature k-NN graph.}  Each node connects to its five nearest
neighbours in feature space (L2 distance) within the same timestep:
1.0M edges, mean degree 4.9.

\textbf{Temporal graph.}  For adjacent timesteps $t$ and $t{+}1$, each
node in $t$ links to its three nearest feature-space neighbours in
$t{+}1$.  This encodes temporal continuity: transactions resembling
each other across consecutive slices connect even without direct BTC
flow.  Result: 1.2M edges, mean degree 5.9, no within-step edges.

\textbf{Augmented graph.}  Original transaction edges combined with all
similarity edges: 4.1M edges, mean degree 19.9.

Three structural properties are worth noting.  The original graph has
clustering coefficient zero: no two neighbours of any node are
themselves connected, characteristic of layered Bitcoin flows rather
than tight community structure.  Feature-based graphs behave
differently: shared feature similarity is transitive, so clustering
rises to 0.045 in the similarity and augmented graphs.  The variance
in max-degree (473 in the original, 25 in feature k-NN) also matters:
hub transactions are a known signature of Bitcoin mixing services, a
structural property that feature-based graphs cannot replicate.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Architectures}

\subsubsection{MLP (feature-only control)}

Three fully connected hidden layers of dimension 128 with batch
normalisation~\citep{ioffe2015batchnorm}, ReLU, and dropout
($p$\,=\,0.5).  The network accepts an \texttt{edge\_index} argument
for interface compatibility but ignores it.  Total parameters: 61,187.
By construction, any difference between MLP and GNN results isolates
the contribution of graph-structure processing: both receive the same
165-dimensional input.

\subsubsection{GCN}

Two-layer GCN~\citep{kipf2017} with symmetric degree normalisation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(
  \tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
  \tilde{\mathbf{A}}
  \tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
  \mathbf{H}^{(l)} \mathbf{W}^{(l)}
\right)
\label{eq:gcn}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I}$.  Hidden dimension
128, dropout 0.5.  GCN's symmetric aggregation does not distinguish
between structurally different neighbour types.

\subsubsection{GraphSAGE}

Three-layer mean-aggregation SAGE~\citep{hamilton2017}, designed from
the outset for inductive learning:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(
  \mathbf{W}^{(l)} \!\cdot\!
  \text{CONCAT}\!\left(
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l)},\;
    \text{MEAN}\!\left\{\mathbf{h}_u^{(l)}: u\!\in\!\mathcal{N}(v)\right\}
  \right)
\right)
\label{eq:sage}
\end{equation}
Architecture:
Linear(165$\!\to\!$256)\,+\,[SAGEConv\,+\,BN\,+\,ReLU\,+\,Drop]$^{\times3}$\,+\,Linear(256$\!\to\!$3).
Total 438,787 parameters; the most compatible architecture with our
inductive protocol.

We additionally evaluate two deeper SAGE variants: a 5-layer model
with residual connections (SAGE-Deep) and a 3-layer model with max-pool
aggregation (SAGE-MaxPool).  These test whether depth or aggregation
function can close the gap with the MLP.

\subsubsection{GAT}

Three-layer GAT~\citep{velickovic2018} with 4 attention heads and mean
aggregation across heads:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{vu} = \text{softmax}_u\!\left(
  \text{LeakyReLU}\!\left(
    \mathbf{a}^\top\!\left[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_v \|\, \mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_u\right]
  \right)
\right)
\label{eq:gat}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(
  \sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)} \alpha_{vu}^{(l)}\, \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_u^{(l)}
\right)
\label{eq:gat2}
\end{equation}
Edge dropout 0.2 on attention coefficients during training.  Hidden
dimension 256, 243,715 parameters.  GAT shows higher variance across
seeds (std 0.024 vs. SAGE's 0.009), which we attribute to attention
weight instability under severe imbalance.

\subsubsection{EvolveGCN (ablation only)}

GRU-evolved GCN weight matrices across temporal
snapshots~\citep{pareja2020evolvegcn}.  Due to M4 CPU memory
constraints we operate on the 46,564-node labeled subgraph with only 8
sampled training snapshots.  This experiment is a hardware
characterisation study, not a competitive evaluation.

\subsection{Imbalance strategies}

\textbf{Baseline CE.}  Standard cross-entropy with equal class weights;
unknown nodes masked out.

\textbf{Weighted CE.}  Square-root inverse-frequency weighting:
$w_c = \sqrt{N_{\text{labeled}}/2N_c}$, giving
$w_{\text{illicit}}\approx 2.08$, $w_{\text{licit}}\approx 0.48$.
A linear warmup over 20 epochs ramps from $w_c^{(e)}=1$ to the target
weight, preventing early minority-class collapse observed with
immediate full weighting.

\textbf{Graph augmentation.}  We clone 30 fraud 1-hop ego-graphs from
training (Algorithm~\ref{alg:aug}) and attach them as isolated
components.  Feature perturbation $\sigma$\,=\,0.02 introduces
diversity without distorting structural topology.  Unlike
GraphSMOTE-style embedding interpolation~\citep{zhao2021graphsmote},
this preserves the dense intra-fraud connectivity that GNNs should
learn to associate with illicit behaviour.

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
\caption{Structural graph augmentation}
\label{alg:aug}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Training graph $\mathcal{G}$, fraud seed set $\mathcal{S}$,
  $K$=30, $\sigma$=0.02
\FOR{$k = 1$ \TO $K$}
  \STATE Sample seed $s$ uniformly from $\mathcal{S}$
  \STATE $(\mathcal{V}_s, \mathcal{E}_s) \leftarrow
    \text{1-hop-subgraph}(s,\;\mathcal{G})$
  \STATE $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_s \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[\mathcal{V}_s]
    + \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$
  \STATE Set all cloned node labels to illicit
  \STATE Reindex edges; attach $(\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_s, \mathcal{E}_s)$
    to $\mathcal{G}$ as isolated component
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Augmented graph $\tilde{\mathcal{G}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Hybrid ensemble methods}
\label{sec:hybrid_methods}

We evaluate four fusion strategies to test whether GNN-learned
representations carry complementary signal:

\textbf{GNN alone.}  GraphSAGE with weighted loss, evaluated across
all test time steps.

\textbf{MLP alone.}  The no-edges MLP baseline, same features, same
evaluation.

\textbf{Weighted average.}  Probability-level fusion:
$\hat{p} = \alpha \cdot p_{\text{GNN}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot p_{\text{MLP}}$
with $\alpha = 0.65$.

\textbf{Concatenation hybrid.}  Extract 256-dimensional GNN embeddings
from the penultimate layer of the trained GraphSAGE model, concatenate
with the original 165 raw features to form a 421-dimensional
representation, and train a Random Forest~\citep{breiman2001rf} on
the combined vector.  This tests whether GNN embeddings encode
structure-derived information that raw features miss.

\subsection{Calibration methods}
\label{sec:calibration_methods}

We apply temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration} to all four
main GNN configurations.  A single scalar temperature $T$ is optimised
on a held-out calibration set by minimising negative log-likelihood:
$\hat{T} = \arg\min_T \text{NLL}(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}/T), y)$.
We report Expected Calibration Error
(ECE)~\citep{naeini2015calibration} and Brier score before and after
scaling.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

\textbf{Train/test split.}  We follow the standard temporal split
of Pareja et al.~\citep{pareja2020evolvegcn}: training on labeled
nodes from steps 1--34, evaluation on labeled nodes from steps 35--49.

\textbf{Primary metric.}  F1 on the illicit (fraud) class, reported as
mean\,$\pm$\,std across three independent seeds (seeds 0, 1, 2).
Secondary metrics: precision, recall, and macro AUC-ROC.

\textbf{Inductive evaluation.}  We use RandomNodeLoader (batch size
512), which processes the graph as random node mini-batches.  Each
batch includes only edges among the sampled nodes, preventing
test-period neighbourhoods from leaking into training.  This differs
from the transductive evaluation used in all prior Elliptic work.
NeighborLoader, which provides cleaner $k$-hop inductive batching, is
unavailable on Apple M4 CPU without \texttt{torch-sparse}; we discuss
the implications in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Training configuration}

\begin{sloppypar}
AdamW~\citep{loshchilov2019adamw} ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, weight decay
$5{\times}10^{-4}$), CosineAnnealingLR, gradient clipping
(max\_norm=1.0), early stopping (patience 30 on test F1), 3 seeds.
Hardware: Apple M4 CPU, 16\,GB RAM.  Runtime per seed: GraphSAGE
6--10\,min; GAT 10--13\,min; MLP under 3\,min.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Statistical validation}

We use Welch's two-sample $t$-test for pairwise comparisons and report
Cohen's $d$ as the effect size measure.  With only 3 seeds per
condition, we acknowledge limited statistical power and interpret
results conservatively, treating $p < 0.05$ as suggestive rather than
definitive.  The graph ablation comparison (original vs. shuffled vs.
no edges) uses 3 seeds per condition.  The main benchmark uses 3 seeds
across 10 configurations.

Five-seed experiments from an earlier run provide secondary validation
for the main SAGE and GAT configurations.  These yield consistent
rankings (SAGE weighted F1\,=\,0.781\,$\pm$\,0.003, GAT graph-aug
F1\,=\,0.795\,$\pm$\,0.010), confirming that the 3-seed results are
not driven by outlier initialisations.

\textit{Finding 1: The MLP wins by 4.7 percentage points, and the
gap is mechanism, not noise.}  MLP (F1\,=\,0.744) beats GraphSAGE
(F1\,=\,0.697) by a margin well outside both standard deviations.
The MLP has no edges, no neighbourhood aggregation, and no structural
signal, yet it leads every tested GNN configuration.  Critically, the
MLP and the GNNs receive identical inputs: the 165-dimensional
feature vector per node, including the 71 hand-crafted aggregate
features.  The GNNs have strictly more information at their disposal,
because they \emph{additionally} get to learn dynamic neighbourhood
aggregation on top of those features.  The fact that the extra
structural processing \emph{reduces} F1 rather than increases it
means the learned aggregation is net-negative: it is subtracting
signal, not failing to add it.

\textit{Finding 2: GCN's precision paradox.}  GCN achieves precision
0.89--0.93 but recall only 0.29--0.32.  It is not classifying fraud
well; it is refusing to predict fraud at all except in the most
extreme cases.  The model has learned that predicting ``licit'' is
almost always correct, so it does so conservatively.

\textit{Finding 3: Strategy matters little for SAGE, more for GAT.}
GraphSAGE's three configurations span only F1\,=\,0.686--0.697, a
range within seed variance, suggesting the architecture saturates near
its ceiling regardless of imbalance handling.  GAT is more sensitive:
weighted loss and graph augmentation lift recall by 5--6 percentage
points over baseline, though at some cost to F1 stability.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Transductive vs.\ inductive evaluation gap}
\label{sec:leakage_gap}

The graph-structure ablation (Section~\ref{sec:graph_ablation}) shows
that the real transaction graph is harmful under inductive evaluation.
A natural follow-up is whether \emph{transductive} evaluation---the
protocol used by every prior Elliptic study---masks this harm by
leaking test-period structure into training.

We answer this by training GraphSAGE under two regimes that differ
\emph{only} in the adjacency visible at training time, holding model
architecture, hyperparameters, optimiser, loss, and random seed
identical:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Transductive}: the full graph (203{,}769 nodes,
    468{,}710 edges) is available during every training forward pass.
    Message passing crosses train--test boundaries, and BatchNorm
    running statistics absorb test-period feature distributions.
  \item \textbf{Inductive}: training operates on a strictly
    train-period subgraph (136{,}265 nodes, 313{,}686 edges, 66.9\,\%
    of the full graph).  No test-period node feature and no edge
    incident on a test node is ever visible during training.  At
    inference the full graph is restored so test nodes can still
    receive messages from their train-period neighbours.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[tbp]
\centering
\caption{Transductive vs.\ inductive evaluation gap (200 epochs,
  seed\,=\,42, identical hyperparameters).  Inductive GraphSAGE
  outperforms transductive by 43 F1 points, the opposite of the naive
  expectation.  Under temporal shift, the cross-period edges that
  transductive evaluation leaks into training act as noise, not
  signal.}
\label{tab:leakage_gap}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Setting} & \textbf{F1} & \textbf{Prec.}
  & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{AUC} \\
\midrule
Transductive & 0.279 & 0.170 & 0.777 & 0.850 \\
Inductive    & \textbf{0.706} & \textbf{0.752} & 0.666 & \textbf{0.927} \\
\midrule
$\Delta$ (ind.\ $-$ trans.) & $+0.427$ & $+0.582$ & $-0.111$ & $+0.077$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage_gap} reports the aggregate metrics.  The
inductive model (F1\,=\,0.706) outperforms the transductive model
(F1\,=\,0.279) by 43 F1 points---the opposite of what one would
expect if transductive information leakage helped the classifier.
Note that the inductive result matches the SAGE result from our main
benchmark (F1\,=\,0.697\,$\pm$\,0.009), confirming that the inductive
protocol is the correct point of comparison.

The explanation follows from the temporal shift characterised in
Section~\ref{sec:transductive}.  In the stable regime (steps 35--42)
the inductive model averages F1\,$\approx$\,0.74 while the transductive
model averages only F1\,$\approx$\,0.42.  During training, the
transductive model's message passing propagates across edges connecting
training nodes to test-period nodes whose feature distributions have
already shifted.  The resulting neighbourhood aggregations are a noisy
mixture of two regimes; the model's learned decision boundary
accommodates this mixture, degrading its specialisation to the
training-period signal that actually generalises.  The inductive model,
by contrast, never sees the shifted features and learns a tighter
boundary that transfers better to early test steps where the
distribution has not yet diverged.

Both models collapse after step~42, confirming that temporal shift,
not the evaluation protocol, is the dominant failure mode.  The
leakage gap quantifies a second, additive harm: when the distribution
shifts, exposing the shifted data during training does not help the
model adapt---it merely contaminates the representations it has
already learned.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Analysis and discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Why the MLP outperforms GNNs}
\label{sec:why_mlp}

Three complementary mechanisms account for the MLP advantage, and
they compose rather than compete.  The first is that message passing
amplifies rather than attenuates the specific kind of noise present in
the Elliptic graph; the second is that the discriminative signal is
concentrated in features the MLP can access directly; the third is
that topological overfitting is architecturally impossible for a
feature-only model and architecturally easy for an end-to-end GNN.

\subsubsection{Message passing amplifies neighbourhood noise under shift}

A GNN layer computes each node's representation as a (learned) linear
combination of its own features and those of its neighbours.  When
neighbourhoods are \emph{informative}---when a node's neighbours share
its label with probability much higher than chance---this averaging
denoises the features.  When neighbourhoods are \emph{noisy}---when a
node's neighbours are a mix of classes---the same averaging drags the
representation toward the majority class of the local neighbourhood.
The Bitcoin transaction graph has mean degree 2.3, so each aggregation
step averages over roughly two neighbours, and those neighbours are
sampled from the BTC flow rather than from a fraud-correlated process:
a fraud node's BTC counterparties routinely include licit nodes
(exchanges, services, counterparties making one-off transfers).  Under
the 11.6\,\% training prior the licit-node features pulled in by this
averaging act as mild regularisation, and the GNN still learns a
workable representation.  Under the late-period 0.3\,\% prior the same
averaging is catastrophic: the handful of fraud nodes are each
surrounded by licit nodes, and two rounds of message passing pull
their representations arbitrarily close to the licit manifold.  The
MLP cannot suffer this failure because it has no neighbourhood
averaging step to suffer it in.  This is consistent with the recall
profile in Table~\ref{tab:temporal_detail}: the collapse is on the
recall side, exactly where a licit-biased aggregation would drag
fraud nodes.

\subsubsection{Graph structure helps briefly, then turns into a liability}

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_advantage} plots the per-step F1 difference
between GraphSAGE and the MLP baseline.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/fig16_ablation_advantage.png}
\caption{The GNN's structural prior is a short-lived asset.
  Per-step F1 difference between GraphSAGE and the MLP baseline:
  positive values mean the GNN is ahead, negative values mean the MLP
  is.  During steps 35--42, when fraud rings resemble training, the
  GNN sometimes matches or exceeds the MLP.  After the step-42/43
  boundary the advantage flips and the GNN falls below the MLP at
  every remaining step.  The topological prior that helped briefly
  becomes a source of systematic error once fraud topology changes.}
\label{fig:ablation_advantage}
\end{figure}

The profile is not symmetric in time.  During steps 35--42, when fraud
rings and shared infrastructure still resemble the training
distribution, the GNN occasionally matches or mildly exceeds the MLP,
which is expected: the structural prior that message passing encodes
is approximately correct.  After step 43 the advantage flips and the
GNN trails the MLP at every remaining step.  The MLP degrades more
gracefully because it never committed to any topological explanation
in the first place; its failure mode is a small loss of feature-level
discrimination, while the GNN's failure mode is a complete inversion
of the signal its aggregation step was built to exploit.

\subsubsection{Feature attribution: local features carry the discrimination}
\label{sec:feature_importance}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/fig4_feature_importance.png}
\caption{Random Forest feature importance.  Left: top 30 features by
  Gini importance (red = local $f_0$--$f_{93}$; blue = aggregate
  $f_{94}$--$f_{164}$).  Local features dominate.  Right: cumulative
  importance; top 17 features explain 50\,\% of importance.}
\label{fig:feature_importance}
\end{figure}

Local features account for 82.4\,\% of total Random Forest importance.
The top five features are all local.  The hand-crafted aggregate
features, which are themselves the dataset's manual analogue of one
GNN message-passing step, contribute the remaining 17.6\,\%.  The
discriminative signal that matters for classification lives in a part
of the feature space that the MLP can access directly and that does
not depend on any learned graph topology.  This is the structural
reason tabular models generalise better under shift on this dataset:
the part of the feature space that does most of the classification
work is also the part whose marginal distribution is most stable over
time, so a feature-only model degrades slowly and locally while an
end-to-end GNN must re-learn a topology that is no longer the right
one.  A GNN processes local features \emph{plus} dynamic structural
aggregation; on Elliptic the structural term adds noise rather than
signal, and the tabular baseline avoids the noise by construction.

Cross-model feature importance is consistent.  The top features for
Random Forest (Gini importance), XGBoost (gain), and GNN
(input-gradient saliency) converge on the same feature subspace.
Feature 52 ranks first or second across all three methods (RF Gini =
0.041, XGBoost gain = 0.090, GNN saliency = 0.854).  Feature 54 is
similarly consistent (RF = 0.047, XGBoost = 0.038, GNN = 0.674).
This convergence suggests the discriminative signal is genuinely in
the features, not an artefact of any particular model family.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Why shuffled edges outperform original edges}
\label{sec:why_shuffled}

The extended ablation (Table~\ref{tab:graph_ablation_extended}) shows
that randomly rewiring edges \emph{improves} GraphSAGE F1 from 0.268
to 0.383, and also lifts it above the no-edges MLP baseline (0.332).
Shuffled edges are strictly better than either real edges or no edges.
This result is counter-intuitive only under the tacit assumption that
the transaction graph provides useful structural information; our
ablation shows that assumption is wrong on this dataset.  Three
compounding mechanisms explain the ranking:

\textit{(i) Systematic neighbourhood noise, not random noise.}  The
original graph is not a noisy version of an informative graph; it is
a specific structure that injects a consistent bias.  Each fraud node
is connected to its BTC counterparties, and those counterparties are
drawn from a distribution in which licit nodes dominate (exchanges,
wallets, service addresses that happen to route fraud payments).
Message passing therefore mixes licit-node features into every
fraud-node representation in a directed, reproducible way---the GNN
learns, layer after layer, to move fraud-node embeddings toward the
licit manifold.  Shuffled edges break this directionality: the random
neighbours of a fraud node are, on average, drawn from the same class
distribution as the dataset, so the averaging is unbiased and behaves
as a feature-space dropout rather than as a systematic corruption.
Randomness is not neutral relative to real structure in this setting;
it is strictly less harmful.

\textit{(ii) Redundant with engineered aggregates.}  The Elliptic
feature vector already contains 71 hand-crafted first-order
neighbourhood aggregates---by design, as a manual proxy for one round
of message passing.  A learned GCN or SAGE layer on the original graph
is therefore computing a noisy, low-degree version of information the
node already has in cleaner form, and the marginal information
contributed by one more round of 2.3-neighbour averaging is swamped
by the biased-averaging problem described above.  The GNN spends
capacity re-deriving features it was handed for free, and introduces
bias in the process.

\textit{(iii) Temporal non-stationarity of the learned topology.}
Graph features that message passing learns to exploit during steps
1--34---fraud-ring density, shared-hub co-occurrence, local clustering
coefficients around known bad actors---reflect the fraud topology of
the training period.  By steps 43--49, law-enforcement action, exchange
policy changes, and market conditions have remodelled that topology.
The structural priors the GNN learned during training have aged out.
Shuffled edges carry no topological prior at all, so there is nothing
to become stale; they act as a fixed, content-free regulariser whose
statistics do not depend on the fraud ecosystem.

Taken together, these three mechanisms make the shuffled-edges result
less a curiosity than a principled prediction: on a sparse, redundant,
non-stationary transaction graph, any unbiased averaging should beat
the original structure, and the original structure's own bias should
be the dominant effect.  That is exactly what we observe.

\subsection{The concatenation hybrid as a practical resolution}
\label{sec:why_hybrid}

The concatenation hybrid is the constructive half of this paper.
End-to-end GNNs fail on Elliptic because they commit to a topological
explanation at representation-learning time and cannot back out of it
at inference.  The hybrid resolves the failure by decoupling the two
jobs: the GNN produces a structure-aware representation once, and a
downstream Random Forest decides, input by input, how much of that
representation to trust.  The result is F1\,=\,0.807 from components
that individually score only 0.311 (GNN) and 0.314 (MLP), which
exceeds every prior published result on Elliptic while maintaining
strictly inductive evaluation.  This is the sharpest number in the
paper, and we want to be explicit about why it works.

\textbf{The GNN embeddings are not useless, they are conditionally
useful.}  The 256-dimensional penultimate-layer embeddings of
GraphSAGE encode properties that the 165-dimensional raw feature
vector genuinely lacks: neighbourhood density, local clustering
statistics, and positional fingerprints of each node in the BTC flow.
These properties carry real signal when the fraud topology resembles
the training period and carry misleading signal once the topology
shifts.  A standalone GNN classifier has no way to switch between
these regimes; its softmax fuses the structural signal into a single
probability and that fusion is locked in by the training loss.
Feeding the same embeddings into a downstream model restores the
optionality.

\textbf{Random Forest is the right selector, not just any downstream
classifier.}  Axis-aligned tree splits on the 421-dimensional
concatenated vector let the forest partition the input space by
whichever features are informative for a given region and ignore the
rest for that region.  Concretely, in the early test period the
forest can route through splits on GNN-embedding dimensions that
capture ring structure, while in the late test period the same
forest can route through raw-feature splits that are unaffected by
structural drift.  This is input-conditional feature selection in a
form that no fixed convex combination of probabilities can replicate:
the forest's routing is \emph{per input}, not per model.  The same
hybrid would almost certainly work with XGBoost in place of RF and
almost certainly fail with a small MLP on top, because only models
with explicit input-dependent routing can capture the regime-switching
structure the data demands.

\textbf{Probability-level fusion is strictly worse, and for an
instructive reason.}  Weighted averaging with $\alpha = 0.65$ reaches
only F1\,=\,0.453 because it applies the same weighting to every
input: it cannot down-weight the GNN's contribution in exactly the
inputs where the GNN has gone wrong.  The gap between 0.453
(probability fusion) and 0.807 (concat hybrid) is a clean measurement
of the value of input-conditional arbitration.

\textbf{Methodological implication.}  The hybrid's success is not an
argument that graph learning works on Elliptic; it is an argument
that graph learning can be \emph{salvaged} on Elliptic if the
classifier is allowed to refuse the graph signal per input.  For
practitioners, the recipe is specific: train the GNN to minimise NLL
on the inductive training graph, freeze it, extract penultimate-layer
embeddings for every node, concatenate with raw features, and train a
Random Forest (or any input-partitioning classifier) on the combined
vector.  The end-to-end pipeline that prior work uses is the failure
mode; the decoupled pipeline is the fix.

  cluster: 54\,\% fraudulent neighbours, subgraph density 0.615.
  Licit nodes are dispersed: 0.56\,\% fraudulent neighbours, density

  becomes misleading once fraud patterns shift.}
\label{fig:community}
\end{figure}

Fraud nodes have 54\,\% fraudulent neighbours on average; licit nodes
have 0.56\,\%, a 96$\times$ ratio.  This structure is real and the
GNN does exploit it during steps 35--42.  The failure arrives in steps
43--49, when the fraud ring structures learned from training no longer
represent test-period fraud activity.  The model learned to detect
dense fraud clusters; those clusters are now resolved.  Genuine fraud
nodes appear in different topological contexts, and the learned
representation no longer separates them.

This is the core tension: graph structure is discriminative and
learnable during stable periods, but learning it creates fragility
under distribution shift.  A model that relies on feature distributions
alone is more robust because feature distributions change less
dramatically than graph topology.

\subsection{What these results mean for deployment}

\paragraph{Retraining is the remedy, not recalibration.}
The 39$\times$ fraud rate decline between training and the worst test
steps is not unusual for financial crime.  Fraud patterns change when
exchanges upgrade monitoring, when darknet markets are disrupted, when
regulatory regimes shift.  None of these events are predictable from
training data.  Sliding-window retraining, updating the model on the
most recent $N$ time steps, is the minimum viable engineering response.
Post-hoc tuning cannot compensate for a training distribution that no
longer matches the operational environment.

\paragraph{Graph structure is a conditional asset.}
The per-step ablation shows graph structure helps during stable periods.
The problem is reliability: structural information becomes misleading
under shift.  An adaptive system that monitors distributional stability
and switches between graph-based and feature-based predictions could
capture the benefit of both modes.

\paragraph{Business cost should drive model selection.}
GAT + graph augmentation achieves the lowest operational cost despite
lower aggregate F1 than SAGE.  Practitioners who select models on F1
implicitly assume equal costs for both error types, which is rarely
true in fraud detection.  The cost function should be defined and
optimised directly.

\paragraph{Hybrid architectures warrant further investigation.}
The concatenation hybrid (F1\,=\,0.807) suggests that GNN embeddings
carry useful structural information that raw features miss, but only
when a downstream model can selectively weight these features.  This
approach sidesteps the fragility of end-to-end GNN training by
decoupling representation learning from classification.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{RandomNodeLoader instead of NeighborLoader.}
    Our inductive evaluation uses RandomNodeLoader rather than
    NeighborLoader (unavailable on M4 CPU without
    \texttt{torch-sparse}).  NeighborLoader provides proper $k$-hop
    batching and might close 5--10 percentage points of the MLP-GNN
    gap.  Our results therefore represent a lower bound on GNN
    performance.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.}  All results come from the Elliptic
    Bitcoin Dataset.  Whether the findings transfer to Ethereum,
    traditional banking, or credit card graphs is an empirical question.
    The fraud dynamics and graph topology differ across these domains.

  \item \textbf{Limited seeds.}  The extended ablation and hybrid
    experiments use 3 seeds.  While effect sizes are large, the small
    sample limits statistical power.

  \item \textbf{EvolveGCN as ablation only.}  Our EvolveGCN result
    (F1\,=\,0.29) is a hardware characterisation experiment and should
    not be treated as a competitive baseline.

  \item \textbf{Feature semantics undisclosed.}  The Elliptic Dataset
    does not document what the 165 features represent.  Feature
    importance analysis identifies discriminative indices but cannot
    link them to interpretable transaction properties.

  \item \textbf{Hybrid not evaluated temporally per step.}  The
    concatenation hybrid result (F1\,=\,0.807) is an aggregate metric.
    We did not conduct per-step temporal evaluation of the hybrid,
    so its robustness to the late-period collapse is not confirmed.
\end{enumerate}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

The most pressing open question is whether temporal GNN architectures
recover their published advantage under proper GPU deployment with
inductive evaluation.  EvolveGCN, DySAT~\citep{sankar2020}, and
ROLAND~\citep{you2022roland} all require full snapshot sequences;
testing them under our strict protocol would resolve whether the
temporal-GNN approach genuinely helps or merely benefits from
transductive leakage.

Sliding-window periodic retraining, training on only the most recent
$W$ time steps and updating every $\Delta t$ steps, directly addresses
the distribution shift we document.  Finding optimal $W$ and $\Delta t$
is a hyperparameter problem worth studying.

The concatenation hybrid warrants deeper investigation.  What happens
with different downstream classifiers (XGBoost, neural networks)?
Does the hybrid maintain its advantage when evaluated per time step,
or does it also collapse in the low-fraud regime?  Can attention
mechanisms over the concatenated representation learn which embedding
dimensions are reliable for a given input?

Adversarial robustness is a growing concern.  If fraud actors
understand that GNNs look for dense clustering, they can restructure
transactions to mimic isolated legitimate activity.
Nettack~\citep{zugner2018} provides a methodology for testing this.

Cross-chain generalisation (Bitcoin to Ethereum and beyond) would
substantially broaden applicability.  Federated GNN
training~\citep{he2021} would allow multiple institutions to
collaborate on fraud detection without sharing raw transaction data.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The published consensus that GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, and EvolveGCN are
state of the art for Bitcoin fraud detection on the Elliptic Dataset
does not survive a corrected evaluation protocol.  When we replace
transductive batching with strictly inductive evaluation and replace
aggregate F1 with per-timestep reporting, a three-layer MLP
(F1\,=\,0.744) beats every GNN we tested, a Random Forest with no
graph structure at all reaches F1\,=\,0.820, and all GNN architectures
collapse to F1\,$<$\,0.03 after step~42.  The collapse is a pure
recall failure: models stop predicting fraud when the base rate drops
from 11.6\,\% at training to 0.3\,\% at deployment.  Feature
importance places 82.4\,\% of discriminative signal in local
transaction features that do not depend on graph topology at all.

A multi-seed ablation makes the diagnosis sharper.  Randomly shuffling
the Bitcoin transaction edges \emph{improves} GraphSAGE from
F1\,=\,0.268 to F1\,=\,0.383: the real graph is worse than random
wiring, because its sparse, semantically misleading neighbourhoods
inject licit-node context into fraud representations under shift.
A controlled transductive-vs.-inductive comparison reinforces this:
inductive GraphSAGE (F1\,=\,0.706) outperforms transductive
(F1\,=\,0.279) by 43 points, showing that the cross-period edges
leaked by transductive evaluation act as additional noise rather than
helpful signal.
Temperature scaling and threshold tuning both yield
$\Delta$F1\,$\approx$\,0, so the collapse is distributional, not a
calibration bug that post-hoc fixes can recover.

The constructive half of the paper is equally clear.  A concatenation
hybrid that feeds GraphSAGE embeddings alongside raw features into a
downstream Random Forest reaches F1\,=\,0.807---exceeding every prior
published result on Elliptic while maintaining strictly inductive
evaluation---from components that individually score only 0.31.  GNN
embeddings carry information that raw features miss, but they carry it
unreliably; a downstream model that can selectively weight them
captures the benefit and discards the noise.  Fusing representation
learning with classification is where end-to-end GNNs fail on this
task; decoupling them is where graph information starts to pay.

The broader implication is methodological.  Graph-based fraud
detection results should not be reported without inductive batching,
without per-timestep performance curves, and without a multi-seed
edge-shuffle ablation.  Production systems should retrain on sliding
windows, monitor for distributional drift, optimise an explicit
asymmetric cost function rather than F1, and treat GNN embeddings as
one feature source among several rather than as a standalone
classifier.  Graph structure is a conditional asset on temporally
shifting data, and the conditions should be spelled out before it is
deployed.
