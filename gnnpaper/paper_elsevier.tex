\title{When Graph Structure Becomes a Liability: A Comprehensive
Benchmark of GNN Architectures for Temporal Fraud Detection on

\ead{saket.maganti@vitap.ac.in}

\begin{abstract}
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a popular choice for financial
fraud detection, largely because transaction networks carry structural
signals---fraud rings cluster densely, layering schemes form distinctive
chains---that individual node features cannot capture alone. Yet this
paper shows that the assumed advantage of GNNs does not hold once
evaluation is conducted rigorously across time.

We benchmark four architectures---MLP, GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT---against
three class-imbalance strategies on the Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset
(203,769 nodes, 234,355 edges, 49 temporal time steps), applying
inductive mini-batch evaluation with three-seed statistical validation,
classical baseline comparisons, and thirteen targeted analyses.
A Random Forest trained on the same temporal split achieves
F1\,=\,0.674, already exceeding the published transductive Random
Forest baseline (F1\,=\,0.640) from the original Elliptic paper. A
plain three-layer MLP achieves F1\,=\,0.744\,$\pm$\,0.005, beating
every GNN. GraphSAGE, the strongest graph model, reaches
F1\,=\,0.697\,$\pm$\,0.009, a gap of 4.7 percentage points.

The explanation lies in temporal distribution shift. Between the
training window (steps 1--34, 11.6\,\% illicit) and the late test
window (steps 43--49), the fraud rate falls as low as 0.3\,\%---a
39-fold reduction. Every tested model collapses from
F1\,$\approx$\,0.95 at step 35 to F1\,$<$\,0.02 after step 43.
The collapse is a \emph{recall} failure: models stop predicting
fraud almost entirely, with the predicted fraud rate dropping from
8.2\,\% to 1.7\,\%.

Random Forest feature attribution reveals that 82.4\,\% of
discriminative power comes from local, transaction-level features
that stay statistically stable across the temporal split. This directly
explains why the MLP is competitive: it relies on exactly those stable
features, while GNNs additionally learn topological patterns from early
time steps that grow misleading as fraud behaviour evolves. Fraud
community structure is real and large---fraud nodes show 54\,\%
fraudulent neighbours versus 0.56\,\% for licit nodes, a
96$\times$ ratio---but exploiting it backfires once the fraud
distribution shifts. Temperature scaling and per-step threshold
tuning both leave F1 unchanged ($\Delta$F1\,$\approx$\,0), ruling out
probability miscalibration as the root cause. An EvolveGCN ablation
on CPU (F1\,=\,0.29 versus published GPU result F1\,=\,0.77) establishes
the hardware requirements for temporal architectures. A business cost
analysis under asymmetric error penalties (FN\,=\,\$50k, FP\,=\,\$2k)
shows that the highest-F1 configuration is not always the cheapest to
operate.

Three practical conclusions follow: fraud detection systems require
periodic retraining because static models cannot track rapid fraud-rate
changes; graph structure introduces harmful inductive bias under
distribution shift even when it is genuinely discriminative under stable
conditions; and temporal GNNs need full snapshot sequences and GPU
hardware to deliver their published advantages. Code and checkpoints
are publicly available.
\end{abstract}
